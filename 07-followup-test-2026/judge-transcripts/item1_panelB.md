# Panel B — Strongest Case FOR the Claim

**CLAIM:** "Vitamin K2 (menatetrenone) supplementation reduces fracture risk in elderly patients and stroke/Parkinson's patients with reduced mobility."

**Role:** Build the strongest possible case that this claim IS well-supported. Devil's advocate for the claim being true, using web research. Honest final assessment included at the end.

---

## 1. The strongest affirmative argument, assembled

### 1.1 Mechanistic plausibility is genuinely strong
Vitamin K2 (menaquinone-4 / menatetrenone, MK-4) is a required cofactor for the gamma-glutamyl carboxylase enzyme that carboxylates osteocalcin, the major non-collagenous bone matrix protein produced by osteoblasts. Under-carboxylated osteocalcin (ucOC) is a validated biomarker of poor vitamin K status and has been repeatedly associated with higher fracture risk in observational cohorts independent of bone mineral density (BMD). Menatetrenone reliably and reproducibly lowers ucOC in essentially every trial that has measured it — including trials that were otherwise negative for fracture or BMD endpoints (e.g., JOINT-03, see below). This is one of the most consistent pharmacodynamic effects in the whole osteoporosis-drug literature, and it gives a coherent biological reason to expect a downstream fracture benefit: menatetrenone is doing something real to bone biology, even where the clinical endpoint hasn't always cleared statistical significance.

There is also a coherent story for why immobile elderly, stroke, and Parkinson's disease (PD) patients specifically would be expected to benefit *more* than ambulatory postmenopausal women: reduced mobility, malnutrition, and reduced sun/dietary intake are independently documented (in sources outside the compromised literature discussed in Section 2) to produce combined vitamin D and vitamin K deficiency in stroke and PD populations, and this deficiency correlates with accelerated bone loss on the paretic/hemiplegic side after stroke and with the multifactorial bone loss seen in PD (motor impairment, immobility, weight loss, medication effects). If a nutrient deficiency is part of the causal chain toward fracture in these populations, replacing that nutrient is a mechanistically sound intervention — this is the strongest a priori argument for the claim's neurological-disease clause.

### 1.2 The best surviving, non-retracted RCT: Shiraki et al. 2000
Masataka Shiraki, Yumiko Shiraki, Choju Aoki, Masakazu Miura. "Vitamin K2 (Menatetrenone) Effectively Prevents Fractures and Sustains Lumbar Bone Mineral Density in Osteoporosis." *Journal of Bone and Mineral Research* 15(3), 2000, pp. 515–521.

- Independent Japanese research group (Research Institute and Practice for Involutional Diseases, Nagano Prefecture) — **not** affiliated with the Sato/Hirosaki/Mitate Hospital group discussed in Section 2.
- 241 osteoporotic patients, 24-month randomized open-label trial. Control (n=121) vs. menatetrenone 45 mg/day + calcium (n=120).
- Reported outcome: new clinical fracture incidence was significantly lower in the treated group (roughly 10% vs. 30% in some summaries of this trial; original report gives χ²=10.935, p=0.0273 for fracture incidence), and lumbar BMD was sustained in the treated group while it continued to decline in controls.
- This is the most-cited single positive RCT in the menatetrenone/fracture literature and, as far as this search could determine, has not been retracted or flagged for integrity concerns.
- Caveat for the "strongest case" framing: open-label design (not double-blind), single research group, relatively small/moderate size — but it is genuine, available evidence for the general elderly/osteoporotic population.

### 1.3 Meta-analytic support (general elderly/postmenopausal population)
Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses report statistically significant pooled reductions in fracture risk associated with vitamin K2/menatetrenone:

- A 2022 systematic review/meta-analysis (*Frontiers in Public Health*, PMC9403798) reported pooled odds ratios favoring K2 supplementation: **hip fracture OR 0.23 (95% CI 0.12–0.47), vertebral fracture OR 0.40 (95% CI 0.25–0.65), non-vertebral fracture OR 0.19 (95% CI 0.11–0.35)** — all statistically significant and directionally strong.
- Mott et al. 2019 (*Osteoporosis International* 30:1543–1559), an updated systematic review/meta-analysis across ~11,112 participants, reported a reduced fracture rate in postmenopausal/osteoporotic patients with vitamin K supplementation.
- Huang et al. 2015 (*Osteoporosis International* 26:1175–86) similarly reported a positive effect of vitamin K2 on postmenopausal osteoporosis outcomes.
- A separate broader vitamin-K systematic review (PMC9138595) reported OR 0.42 (95% CI 0.27–0.66) for vertebral fracture and OR 0.44 (95% CI 0.23–0.88) for clinical fracture with vitamin K vs. control.
- Menatetrenone modestly but consistently increases lumbar spine BMD in pooled analyses (e.g., P=0.006 across 10 studies in the 2022 Frontiers review, 16-RCT/6,425-subject pool).

Taken together, and read in the most favorable light, this is a real, multiply-replicated statistical signal across several independent meta-analytic teams, not a single fluke result.

### 1.4 Regulatory and real-world standing
Menatetrenone (brand name Glakay, Eisai Co.) is an approved prescription drug for osteoporosis in Japan, alongside eldecalcitol and minodronic acid as Japan-specific anti-osteoporotic agents. Japanese osteoporosis practice guidelines describe it as slightly increasing lumbar BMD and reducing vertebral and non-vertebral fractures via a BMD-independent mechanism (osteocalcin carboxylation), and it remains in real-world prescribing use today, typically as an adjunct in elderly patients or those with documented vitamin K insufficiency who are already on bisphosphonate therapy. A regulator having approved and maintained this indication for over two decades is itself a data point in favor of the claim's general elderly/osteoporosis component, independent of any single trial's flaws.

### 1.5 Subgroup signal even in a "negative" mega-trial
The largest RCT in this literature, the OF study (Inoue/Fujita/Kishimoto et al., *J Bone Miner Metab* 2009;27:66–75; n=4,378, 36-month phase IV trial comparing calcium alone vs. calcium+menatetrenone 15 mg/day), was null on its primary endpoint (new vertebral fracture incidence over 36 months did not differ significantly between arms). However, a post hoc subgroup analysis found a reduced incidence of vertebral fractures specifically among postmenopausal women with a more severe baseline vertebral fracture burden (≥5 prevalent vertebral fractures) — i.e., in patients who most resemble a frail, high-risk elderly population. Advocates for the claim can reasonably argue this points toward menatetrenone being most useful in exactly the frail/high-risk elderly and immobile subgroup the claim is about, even though the trial's primary, unselected-population endpoint was not met.

### 1.6 Best available case for the stroke/PD clause specifically
Setting aside the retracted primary trials (Section 2), independent, non-retracted literature supports the *prerequisite* physiological claims for the stroke/PD clause even though it cannot supply a clean, uncontaminated interventional fracture-reduction RCT:
- Nature *Scientific Reports* (2019) and multiple PD-bone reviews (e.g., *ScienceDirect* 2015, 2017) document that PD patients commonly have combined vitamin D and vitamin K deficiency, driven substantially by immobility, and that this deficiency plausibly contributes to PD-associated bone loss.
- Reduced mobility and malnutrition-driven vitamin K deficiency in post-stroke hemiplegia is a recurring theme across the (non-Sato) neurology/bone literature, supporting the deficiency side of the mechanistic chain.
- If one accepts (a) that vitamin K deficiency is real and common in these populations, and (b) that vitamin K2 repletion improves osteocalcin carboxylation and BMD in the general elderly population (Sections 1.2–1.3), then extending the therapy to reduced-mobility neurological patients is a biologically reasonable, guideline-consistent extrapolation — this is the honest best argument available once the fabricated primary trials are set aside.

## 2. The evidence that most directly supports the claim's stroke/Parkinson's clause is fabricated

This is not a minor caveat; it materially determines how strong the "strongest case" actually is, so it is reported in full rather than buried.

Essentially every trial that purports to show menatetrenone directly reduces fracture incidence *specifically in stroke, Parkinson's disease, or Alzheimer's disease patients* traces back to Yoshihiro Sato (Hirosaki University / Mitate Hospital, Japan), whose body of work is one of the largest research-misconduct cases in modern clinical medicine:

- **Sato Y, Honda Y, Kuno H, Oizumi K. "Menatetrenone ameliorates osteopenia in disuse-affected limbs of vitamin D- and K-deficient stroke patients." *Bone* 1998;23:291–6.** This is the specific study (108 hemiplegic stroke patients, 45 mg/day menatetrenone for 12 months, reported BMD +4.3% hemiplegic side vs. −4.7% in untreated controls, p<0.0001, plus a hip-fracture asymmetry) that most directly appears to support the stroke clause of the claim. **This paper was retracted (Retraction: *Bone* 2019;124:167)** after the corresponding author admitted the clinical trial data were fabricated and that co-authors had been listed without their consent.
- **Sato Y, Honda Y, Kaji M, Asoh T, Hosokawa K, Kondo I, Satoh K. "Amelioration of osteoporosis by menatetrenone in elderly female Parkinson's disease patients with vitamin D deficiency." *Bone* 2002;31(1):114–8.** The single most directly on-point study for the Parkinson's clause. **Retracted** (*Bone* 2018;106:212).
- **Sato Y, Kanoko T, Satoh K, Iwamoto J. "Menatetrenone and vitamin D2 with calcium supplements prevent nonvertebral fracture in elderly women with Alzheimer's disease." *Bone* 2005.** **Retracted** at the corresponding author's request for fabricated data.
- Related same-group papers using risedronate (not menatetrenone, but same fabrication pattern, same claimed populations): "Risedronate therapy for prevention of hip fracture after stroke in elderly women" (*Neurology* 2005;64:811–6, retracted *Neurology* 2016;87:239) and "Risedronate and ergocalciferol prevent hip fracture in elderly men with Parkinson disease" (*Neurology* 2007;68(12):911–5, retracted 2016).
- **Iwamoto J, Sato Y. "Efficacy of Menatetrenone (Vitamin K2) against Non-Vertebral and Hip Fractures in Patients with Neurological Diseases." *Clinical Drug Investigation* 2009;29:471–9.** This is the only meta-analysis specifically pooling neurological-disease (Alzheimer's/stroke/PD) menatetrenone trials — it pooled exactly the three studies above. **Retracted** in 2018 because two of its three constituent RCTs had been retracted for fabrication, invalidating the pooled result.
- A related observational companion study, "Vitamin K deficiency and osteopenia in disuse-affected limbs of vitamin D-deficient elderly stroke patients" (1999), from the same author group, also carries a retraction flag.

Independent investigation found Sato authored/co-authored roughly 33 randomized clinical trials, of which 23+ were retracted following a Hirosaki University misconduct investigation (fabrication and authorship violations confirmed in at least 13 papers, plagiarism in another). Sato died in January 2017; retractions of his papers continued for years afterward as journals worked through his back catalogue.

**Net effect on the claim:** the stroke- and Parkinson's-specific fracture-reduction evidence that would most directly and cleanly support the claim as worded does not currently exist in the legitimate literature. What existed has been withdrawn as fabricated.

## 3. Further complications, even for the general-elderly clause

- **The largest RCT (OF study, n=4,378) was null on its primary endpoint.** Only a post hoc, non-prespecified subgroup showed benefit (Section 1.5) — post hoc subgroup findings are hypothesis-generating, not confirmatory.
- **JOINT-03** (Japanese Osteoporosis Intervention Trial-03; ~1,874 patients total, 2-year RCT), a modern, well-powered, non-Sato trial testing menatetrenone 45 mg/day added to risedronate vs. risedronate alone, found **no fracture benefit** from adding vitamin K2 (incidence rate ratio 1.074, 95% CI 0.811–1.422, p=0.62) and **no BMD difference** between arms, despite menatetrenone producing its usual large reduction in ucOC. This is a clean, credible negative result from a well-conducted, non-contaminated trial, and it is one of the more recent and rigorous data points available.
- **A 2023 RCT** (Archives of Osteoporosis, n=105, vitamin K1 vs. MK-4 45 mg/day vs. placebo added to bisphosphonate) also found no additive fracture-risk benefit from vitamin K2.
- Meta-analyses in this space are themselves heterogeneous and sometimes contradictory: one 2022 review noted that pooling six RCTs on fracture incidence gave a null result (RR 0.96, p=0.65), and only became significant (RR 0.43, p=0.01) after excluding one "heterogeneous" study — a data-driven exclusion that should be treated cautiously.
- Sato's 33 trials were not confined to neurological-disease populations; some contributed to the broader "postmenopausal osteoporosis" menatetrenone literature as well. This raises a real (not fully resolved by this search) possibility that some of the "general elderly" meta-analytic pool cited in Section 1.3 may also contain contaminated trials, beyond the three neuro-specific studies known to have been pooled in the retracted Iwamoto/Sato 2009 meta-analysis. This could not be fully audited trial-by-trial within the scope of this task, but it is a live concern flagged by the broader research-integrity literature on this topic (see Retraction Watch coverage), not merely a hypothetical one.

## 4. Honest final assessment

**How strong is the case, really?**

- **For "elderly patients" (general osteoporotic population), stated broadly:** Weak-to-moderate, genuine but contested support. There is a real, non-retracted positive RCT (Shiraki 2000), several meta-analyses reporting statistically significant pooled fracture-risk reductions, a consistent and biologically coherent biomarker effect (ucOC lowering) in essentially every trial, and two decades of regulatory approval and guideline inclusion in Japan. Against that: the largest RCT (OF study, n=4,378) was null on its primary endpoint, a well-powered modern add-on trial (JOINT-03) found no fracture or BMD benefit, and the overall meta-analytic picture is acknowledged even by sympathetic reviewers as "controversial" and inconsistent across pooling choices. A fair characterization is: *plausible, biologically grounded, and supported by some real positive trials, but not settled, and the largest/best trials skew null.*

- **For the "stroke/Parkinson's patients with reduced mobility" clause specifically, as worded in the claim:** This is where the strongest-case exercise breaks down. The specific, on-point interventional evidence — the studies that would actually let someone say "menatetrenone reduces fracture risk in stroke/PD patients" — consists overwhelmingly of work by Yoshihiro Sato that has been formally retracted for data fabrication, including the single most directly relevant stroke study (Bone 1998), the single most directly relevant PD study (Bone 2002), and the one meta-analysis that pooled neurological-disease trials (Clinical Drug Investigation 2009, itself retracted). No credible, non-retracted RCT demonstrating menatetrenone reduces fracture incidence specifically in stroke or PD populations was found in this search. What remains is indirect: legitimate evidence that these patients have vitamin K deficiency, plus legitimate (mixed) evidence that vitamin K2 helps bone metabolism in elderly people generally. That is a plausible extrapolation, not direct confirming evidence, and it should not be presented as though the clinical trials actually exist and support the claim as stated.

**Bottom line:** The strongest honest case for this claim is a mechanistically coherent, partially evidence-backed argument for elderly/osteoporotic patients in general (real positive trial, real meta-analytic signal, real regulatory standing, alongside real null results in the largest trials), combined with a biologically reasonable but evidentially unsupported extrapolation to stroke/Parkinson's patients — an extrapolation whose direct primary literature has been withdrawn as fabricated. Presenting the claim as written, with stroke/Parkinson's patients called out as a supported subgroup, overstates what the (currently valid) literature shows. The most defensible version of this claim, after removing retracted data, is closer to: "Vitamin K2 (menatetrenone) has some support, alongside contradictory null trials, for modestly reducing fracture risk in elderly osteoporotic patients generally; its specific benefit in stroke/Parkinson's patients with reduced mobility is biologically plausible but lacks any surviving, non-retracted direct clinical evidence."
