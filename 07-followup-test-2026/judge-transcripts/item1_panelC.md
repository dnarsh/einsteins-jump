# Evidence Assessment: Vitamin K2 (Menatetrenone) and Fracture Risk Reduction

**Claim under review:** "Vitamin K2 (menatetrenone) supplementation reduces fracture risk in elderly patients and stroke/Parkinson's patients with reduced mobility."

**Date of assessment:** 2026-09-23

---

## Final Rating: **WEAK to UNRELIABLE** (bifurcated — the two halves of the claim rate differently)

- **General elderly/osteoporosis population:** WEAK — directionally suggestive in older meta-analyses, but the largest and most rigorous trial found no overall effect, and a fraud scandal has invalidated a meaningful share of the underlying evidence base.
- **Stroke/Parkinson's (reduced-mobility) subpopulation:** UNRELIABLE — the specific evidence cited for this subgroup traces almost entirely to studies later retracted for data fabrication. There is currently no credible RCT evidence supporting this part of the claim.

---

## 1. What menatetrenone is

Menatetrenone is the brand name for a pharmaceutical-grade, high-dose synthetic vitamin K2 that is chemically identical to menaquinone-4 (MK-4). It is approved as an anti-osteoporosis drug by Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (typically dosed at 45 mg/day) — it is a prescription drug in Japan, not the low-dose MK-7 supplement sold over the counter in the West. This distinction matters because most of the trial evidence for this claim comes from a specific Japanese clinical and research context that has not been well-replicated elsewhere.

---

## 2. Evidence base for the general elderly/osteoporosis claim

### Key primary trials
- **Shiraki M, Shiraki Y, Aoki C, Miura M. "Vitamin K2 (Menatetrenone) Effectively Prevents Fractures and Sustains Lumbar Bone Mineral Density in Osteoporosis." *J Bone Miner Res.* 2000;15(3):515-521.**
  Open-label RCT, n=241 (120 treated, 121 control), 24 months, 45 mg/day menatetrenone. Found reduced clinical fracture incidence (reported relative risk ~0.45) and sustained lumbar BMD. This trial is **not retracted** and remains the most-cited positive primary trial for this claim. However, it is small, open-label (not blinded), and from a single research group — a design profile associated with inflated effect sizes in the broader osteoporosis-trial literature of this era.

- **Inoue T, Fujita T, Kishimoto H, et al. "Randomized controlled study on the prevention of osteoporotic fractures (OF study): a phase IV clinical study of 15-mg menatetrenone capsules." *J Bone Miner Metab.* 2009;27:66-75.**
  This is the **largest and most rigorous** trial in this literature: n=4,378 postmenopausal women, calcium alone vs. calcium + menatetrenone, 36 months, registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT00165607). **The primary endpoint — new vertebral fracture incidence — showed no significant difference between groups overall.** Only a post-hoc subgroup analysis (women with ≥5 prior vertebral fractures) showed a reduced incidence (RR ≈ 0.61), which is exploratory and hypothesis-generating, not confirmatory. This large, better-designed trial is a major negative counterweight to the smaller positive Shiraki trial.

### Meta-analyses
- **Cockayne S, Adamson J, Lanham-New S, Shearer MJ, Gilbody S, Torgerson DJ. "Vitamin K and the prevention of fractures: systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials." *Arch Intern Med* (now *JAMA Intern Med*). 2006;166(12):1256-1261.**
  Pooled mostly Japanese menatetrenone trials and reported large apparent risk reductions (odds ratios roughly 0.40 for vertebral, 0.23 for hip, 0.19 for nonvertebral fractures). This is the meta-analysis most often cited as "proof" of the claim. **Critically, this analysis has since been flagged as compromised**: a 2018 commentary in *JAMA Internal Medicine* ("Caution of Conclusions of a Meta-analysis Including Problematic Trials," 2018;178(6):875-876, with author reply 2018;178(6):863-864) noted that several of the Japanese trials it pooled were later investigated for data-integrity problems. The original DARE quality appraisal also flagged that the authors' own conclusions were appropriately cautious given evidence limitations — i.e., even the pre-scandal read of this review was hedged.

- **Mott A, Bradley T, Wright K, Cockayne ES, Shearer MJ, Adamson J, et al. "Effect of vitamin K on bone mineral density and fractures in adults: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials." *Osteoporos Int.* 2019;30:1543-1559 (with a published correction).**
  This update (36 trials, ~11,112 participants) was explicitly commissioned because "the integrity of some of the previous evidence has been questioned." Result: vitamin K reduced all clinical fractures in postmenopausal/osteoporotic patients (OR 0.72, 95% CI 0.55-0.95) — **but after excluding high-risk-of-bias studies, the effect was no longer significant (OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.58-1.01)**. The authors' own conclusion: "For post-menopausal or osteoporotic patients there is no evidence that vitamin K affects bone mineral density or vertebral fractures; it may reduce clinical fractures, however the evidence is insufficient to confirm this." A subsequent correction further downgraded pooled estimates after concerns emerged about the Yamaguchi Osteoporosis Prevention Study (YOPS, Ishida & Kawai), one of the included trials.

- More recent 2022-2025 meta-analyses (Frontiers in Public Health 2022; J Bone Miner Metab 2022; Frontiers in Endocrinology 2025) continue to show a similar pattern: modest, sometimes statistically fragile improvements in lumbar spine BMD and biochemical markers (reduced undercarboxylated osteocalcin), but fracture-incidence results that are **not robust to exclusion of individual heterogeneous studies** (e.g., one 2022 review found fracture RR non-significant at 0.96 across 6 RCTs, becoming "significant" at RR 0.43 only after dropping one study — a sign of fragile, exclusion-dependent significance rather than a stable effect).

**Bottom line for the general/elderly claim:** the evidence is genuinely mixed and trending negative as trial quality improves. The largest, best-designed trial (OF study, n=4,378) was null on its primary endpoint. The most favorable meta-analyses lose significance once low-quality or since-scrutinized trials are excluded. This supports a WEAK rating, not a moderate or strong one.

## 3. Evidence base for the stroke/Parkinson's (reduced-mobility) subclaim — this is where it breaks down

This is the more serious problem. The specific evidence usually cited for "menatetrenone prevents fractures in stroke and Parkinson's patients with reduced mobility" traces to a body of work substantially authored or co-authored by **Dr. Yoshihiro Sato**, a Japanese neurologist, in collaboration with Jun Iwamoto.

### The fraud
- Sato was found to have fabricated data across dozens of trials over roughly 15 years, concentrated in exactly this niche: fracture prevention (vitamin D, vitamin K, bisphosphonates) in immobilized/neurological populations (stroke, Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease).
- The fraud was uncovered by Alison Avenell (University of Aberdeen) and colleagues from Scotland/New Zealand, who found statistically impossible similarities between "different" patient cohorts across Sato's trials.
- Retraction counts grew over several years: 12 retractions by late 2016, 14 by April 2017, and eventually numbers reported as high as 43-100+ retractions (sources vary on the exact final count, but all agree it is one of the largest retraction records in the field; a separate misconduct allegation extended to nearly 300 papers across 78 journals by Sato and Iwamoto combined for methodological/ethical problems).
- Sato died in January 2017 during the investigation; circumstances are ambiguous in public reporting.
- Damage persisted post-retraction: Avenell documented that many citing articles did not carry retraction warnings, and Sato's retracted findings were still cited in 88 subsequent systematic reviews/guidelines published 2003-2020 ("zombie citations").

### Specific papers underlying the stroke/PD fracture claim — retracted
- Sato Y, Honda Y, Kaji M, et al. "Amelioration of osteoporosis by menatetrenone in elderly female Parkinson's disease patients with vitamin D deficiency." *Bone.* 2002;31:114-118. **Retracted** (Retraction in *Bone* 2018;106:212).
- Sato Y, Kanoko T, Satoh K, et al. "Menatetrenone and vitamin D2 with calcium supplements prevent nonvertebral fracture in elderly women with Alzheimer's disease." *Bone.* 2005;36:61-68. **Retracted.**
- Sato Y, Fujimatsu Y, Honda Y, et al. "Accelerated bone remodeling in patients with poststroke hemiplegia." *J Stroke Cerebrovasc Dis.* 1998;7(1):58-62. **Retracted** (2019).
- Sato Y, Kuno H, Kaji M, et al. "Increased bone resorption during the first year after stroke." *Stroke.* 1998;29(7):1373-1377. **Retracted** (2019).
- Sato Y, Fujimatsu Y, Kikuyama M, et al. "Influence of immobilization on bone mass and bone metabolism in hemiplegic elderly patients with a long-standing stroke." *J Neurol Sci.* 1998;156(2):205-210. **Retracted** (2024).
- The direct meta-analysis specifically making this claim — **"Efficacy of Menatetrenone (Vitamin K2) against Non-Vertebral and Hip Fractures in Patients with Neurological Diseases"** (*Clin Drug Investig.* 2009;29(7)), which pooled stroke, Parkinson's, and Alzheimer's RCTs and reported large apparent effects (RR 0.13 for non-vertebral fractures, RR 0.14 for hip fractures) — was **RETRACTED by the Editor-in-Chief**, explicitly because two of the three trials it pooled (the Parkinson's and Alzheimer's trials above) were later retracted for data fabrication, "rendering the results of this meta-analysis invalid."

### Real-world consequence
Japan's own 2011 and 2015 clinical guidelines recommending vitamin K for fracture-risk patients were partly built on this body of work; once Sato's studies were removed from the underlying evidence synthesis, the reported benefit became statistically nonsignificant.

### What legitimate, non-retracted evidence remains for this subgroup
Essentially none of substance. A newer, methodologically distinct pilot study — **PD-K2** (a placebo-controlled trial of MK-7, not MK-4/menatetrenone, in genetically-defined Parkinson's disease with mitochondrial dysfunction, ~130 participants) — is underway/recent, but it targets a different outcome (mitochondrial/theranostic markers) and a different vitamin K form, not fracture incidence, so it cannot be used to support the original claim.

**Bottom line for the stroke/Parkinson's subclaim:** the evidentiary chain for this specific, more dramatic part of the claim runs directly through fabricated data. This is not a case of "weak but suggestive" evidence — it is a case where the primary studies were retracted for fraud, and the pooling meta-analysis that synthesized them was itself retracted as a direct consequence. Any contemporary source that still asserts this subclaim without flagging the retractions is propagating a "zombie citation."

## 4. Overall assessment and reasoning

| Evidence component | Status |
|---|---|
| General elderly osteoporosis population, small early RCT (Shiraki 2000) | Legitimate, not retracted, but small/open-label/single-center |
| General elderly osteoporosis population, largest RCT (OF study, n=4,378) | Legitimate, rigorous — **null on primary fracture endpoint** |
| Cockayne 2006 meta-analysis (most-cited "proof") | Legitimate journal record, but built partly on trials later flagged/scrutinized; formally cautioned against in a 2018 JAMA Intern Med commentary |
| Mott 2019 updated meta-analysis | Legitimate, purpose-built to correct for integrity concerns — found fracture benefit **not significant** after excluding high-risk-of-bias trials |
| Stroke/Parkinson's-specific claim | Evidentiary chain retracted for data fabrication (Sato scandal); pooling meta-analysis also retracted |

**Rating: WEAK (general population) / UNRELIABLE (stroke-Parkinson's subpopulation), combined rating for the claim as stated: WEAK-TO-UNRELIABLE.**

Reasoning:
1. Even setting fraud aside, the best-designed, largest trial in the general-population literature (Inoue/OF study, n=4,378) was null on its primary fracture endpoint, and the most rigorous meta-analysis (Mott 2019) explicitly states current evidence is "insufficient to confirm" a clinical-fracture benefit once low-quality trials are excluded.
2. The claim's more specific and clinically striking part — efficacy in stroke and Parkinson's patients with reduced mobility — is not just "weak" but traces directly to one of the largest documented research-fraud scandals in bone-health literature (Yoshihiro Sato), with the specific supporting studies and their pooling meta-analysis formally retracted.
3. This is a textbook case of a claim that likely originated in real (if modest) legitimate science for one population (general elderly osteoporosis) but was extended, via fabricated data, to a second population (stroke/Parkinson's) where no credible trial evidence currently exists. Sources repeating the full claim as stated should be treated as unreliable unless they explicitly address the retractions.
