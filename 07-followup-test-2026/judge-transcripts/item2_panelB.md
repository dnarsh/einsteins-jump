# Panel B — Strongest Case FOR: "Hydroxyethyl starch solutions are a safe and effective choice for fluid resuscitation in critically ill and surgical patients, with outcomes comparable to or better than crystalloid fluids"

Role: build the strongest genuine, evidence-based case FOR this claim. Web research conducted 2026-09-23.

---

## 1. The strongest form of the claim the evidence actually supports

Before presenting the case, one scoping move matters: the best available evidence supports a **narrower but still substantial** version of the claim — modern low-molecular-weight hydroxyethyl starch (HES 130/0.4, "tetrastarch"), used within labeled dosing limits (roughly <30 mL/kg, <24 h), in **non-septic surgical/trauma patients without pre-existing renal impairment**, produces outcomes statistically indistinguishable from — and on several secondary/hemodynamic measures better than — crystalloid, with no reproducible signal of excess mortality or renal failure in this population. That is the version argued below, and it is a serious, well-powered, recently reconfirmed position, not a fringe one.

---

## 2. Tier 1 evidence: the EMA's own requested confirmatory trial found HES safe in surgery

**PHOENICS trial** (Bühre et al., *European Journal of Anaesthesiology*, 2025/2026) is the single most important data point for this case, for two reasons: it is the largest dedicated surgical RCT ever run on this question, and it was **commissioned by the EMA itself** after the 2013/2018 regulatory restrictions, specifically to generate long-term safety evidence in the perioperative population the agency said was under-studied.

- Randomized, double-blind, 53 sites, 10 European countries, 1,985 patients undergoing elective major abdominal surgery with expected blood loss ≥500 mL.
- Primary endpoint (renal function, eGFR change from baseline to minimum): HES 130/0.4 was **statistically non-inferior** to crystalloid (−3.4 ± 17.7 mL/min/1.73m² vs −1.0 ± 17.1 mL/min/1.73m², P<0.001 for non-inferiority).
- Key secondary endpoint — composite of mortality + major postoperative complications at 90 days — was **identical: 35% in both arms**.
- No clinically relevant difference on any safety endpoint, including 90-day renal function.
- 1-year all-cause mortality numerically **favored HES**: 8.6% (HES) vs 10.1% (crystalloid) — not powered to be conclusive on its own, but a trend in the claim's favor, not against it.
- Authors' conclusion: perioperative in-label use of HES is "well tolerated," providing "robust evidence" against the extrapolated harm signal from ICU/sepsis trials.

This directly rebuts the strongest objection to the claim (that regulatory restriction reflects settled harm) — the trial the regulator itself asked for came back supporting safety in the surgical population.

## 3. Tier 2 evidence: large, recent meta-analyses converge on "no renal or mortality signal" in surgical patients, plus hemodynamic advantages

**Sessler, Pradelli, Povero, Chappell, Wang — *Anesthesiology* 2026;145:591–603** (the largest and most current meta-analysis identified):
- 114 RCTs, 13,951 surgical patients (6,753 on HES 130/0.4), search through mid-2025 across six languages.
- Acute kidney injury: RR 1.02 (95% CI 0.91–1.16), P=0.70 — **no signal**.
- Serum creatinine change: −0.62 µmol/L (95% CI −3.82 to 2.57) — **no meaningful worsening**.
- Hypotension: RR 0.79 (95% CI 0.67–0.91), P=0.002 — **significantly favors HES**.
- Vasopressor use: 8–10% relative reduction with HES.
- Blood transfusion: only ~12 mL additional volume — the authors call this **clinically insignificant**, directly countering the older coagulopathy narrative for this modern formulation.
- Authors' direct quote: "Current hydroxyethyl starch solutions do not provoke renal injury in surgical patients," concluding the data "support a more nuanced view of HES use within contemporary dosing frameworks."
- The one caveat the authors themselves flag: risk rises specifically **outside labeled dosing** (>30 mL/kg: RR 1.28, P=0.03; use beyond 24 h: RR 1.33, P=0.05) — which if anything supports the claim's core assertion when HES is used *as labeled*, and simply argues for adherence to dosing limits rather than against the fluid itself.

**Chappell, van der Linden, Ripollés-Melchor, James — *British Journal of Anaesthesia* 2021** ("tetrastarches in surgery and trauma"):
- 85+ RCTs of HES 130/0.4-0.42 vs albumin, gelatin, or crystalloid in surgical/trauma patients.
- HES patients had **lower** postoperative serum creatinine (P<0.001), with no difference in renal dysfunction, renal failure, or need for renal replacement therapy.
- HES **improved hemodynamic stability**, reduced vasopressor requirement (P<0.001), and **decreased length of hospital stay** (P<0.001) versus crystalloid alone.
- Authors' conclusion: "indication- and protocol-based use of tetrastarches in surgery is both safe and efficacious."

**Independent 2021 systematic review, 25 RCTs / 4,111 adult surgical patients (PMC8111748):**
- No significant difference in 30-day mortality (RR 1.28, 95% CI 0.88–1.86), AKI (RR 1.23, 95% CI 0.99–1.53), or need for RRT (RR 0.75, 95% CI 0.37–1.53) between HES 130/0.4 and comparator fluids.
- Findings were **consistent across both cardiac and non-cardiac surgery subgroups** — i.e., the null-harm finding is not an artifact of one surgical population.

Three independently conducted, large-sample analyses — spanning different author teams, time windows, and search strategies — converge on the same conclusion for the surgical population: no reproducible renal or mortality harm, plus a consistent secondary signal of hemodynamic benefit (less vasopressor need, less hypotension, shorter stay).

## 4. Mechanistic/physiological support: the volume-sparing effect is real and measurable, not just theoretical

The Starling-principle rationale for colloids — that iso-oncotic fluid stays intravascular longer and produces a larger, faster plasma-volume expansion than crystalloid, reducing total fluid load and interstitial edema — is not merely theoretical for HES. A prospective observational study in major abdominal surgery (BMC Anesthesiology, 2020) directly measured this: fluid challenge with 6% HES produced a **7.3% increase in plasma volume** at 30 minutes, versus **1.0%** for balanced crystalloid, and the stroke-volume-index response (area under the curve) was significantly larger with HES than with crystalloid. This is consistent with the FIRST trial finding below and gives a plausible physiological mechanism for the hemodynamic-stability and lower-vasopressor-use findings reported across the meta-analyses in Section 3 — it is not an isolated statistical artifact.

## 5. Trauma-specific evidence: the FIRST trial (James et al., *BJA* 2011)

- Randomized, double-blind trial, 109 patients with severe blunt or penetrating trauma requiring >3 L resuscitation, single center (Cape Town).
- In **penetrating trauma**, HES 130/0.4 patients needed significantly less total fluid (5.1 ± 2.7 L vs 7.4 ± 4.3 L with saline, P<0.001), and showed **better lactate clearance** (P<0.05) and **better preserved renal function** (P=0.018) than saline.
- This is a rare trauma trial showing HES outperforming crystalloid on a hard physiological endpoint (renal function), not merely matching it.

## 6. ICU-level colloid evidence (CRISTAL): even in critical illness broadly, colloids (including HES) showed a secondary mortality *advantage*

**CRISTAL trial** (JAMA 2013, 2,857 ICU patients with hypovolemic shock, colloids including HES/gelatin/dextran/albumin vs crystalloids):
- Primary endpoint (28-day mortality): no significant difference (25.4% colloids vs 27.0% crystalloids, P=0.26) — i.e., no harm.
- Secondary endpoint, 90-day mortality, **significantly favored colloids**: 30.7% vs 34.2% (RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.86–0.99, P=0.03).
- Colloid patients had significantly **more days alive without mechanical ventilation** and **more days alive without vasopressor therapy**, at both 7 and 28 days (all P≤0.04).
- No evidence the treatment effect varied over time or across pre-defined subgroups.

This is a large, real-world, pragmatic ICU trial — the closest thing to a head-to-head test of the claim's "critically ill" language at scale — and it leans toward colloids, not away from them, on longer-horizon mortality and organ-support-free days.

## 7. Regulatory context, read favorably: restriction was targeted, not a general condemnation, and the surgical indication was deliberately preserved and then re-tested

The 2013 and 2018 EU regulatory actions are often cited as blanket condemnation of HES, but the actual regulatory history supports a narrower story consistent with the claim above:
- The 2018 CMDh decision explicitly preserved the surgical/trauma indication ("HES solutions may continue to be used in patients to treat hypovolaemia caused by acute blood loss when treatment with crystalloids alone is not sufficient"), restricting only the sepsis/burn/critically-ill/renal-impairment population.
- Regulators' own stated rationale for not fully suspending surgical use was a *lack of evidence of harm* in that population, paired with a request for more data — a request PHOENICS (Section 2) was built to answer, and which came back supportive.
- The label-adherence caveat found in the Sessler 2026 meta-analysis (harm signal only appears above 30 mL/kg or beyond 24 h dosing) maps precisely onto the regulatory risk-minimization measures already put in place (controlled access, accredited hospitals, dosing caps) — i.e., the system designed to keep HES inside its safety margin appears to be working as intended in the surgical population.

## 8. Honest final assessment of how strong this case actually is

This is a genuinely defensible, evidence-backed case for the **surgical/perioperative and trauma-hemorrhage** portion of the claim, built on some of the best available data: a large EMA-commissioned confirmatory RCT (PHOENICS, ~2,000 patients) and the largest meta-analysis in the literature (Sessler 2026, ~14,000 patients), both very recent, both converging with two earlier independent meta-analyses and a positive trauma RCT. The volume-sparing/hemodynamic-stability finding is not just plausible but mechanistically grounded and measured directly. On the ICU/critically-ill side, CRISTAL is real, large, and leans favorable on secondary endpoints (90-day mortality, organ-support-free days), which is more support than the claim's critics typically acknowledge.

That said, intellectual honesty requires stating the limits plainly:

- **The claim as written ("critically ill... patients") overreaches what the best evidence supports.** The 6S trial (severe sepsis, Scandinavia) and CHEST trial found HES associated with increased renal replacement therapy and, in 6S, increased mortality — both large, well-conducted RCTs specifically in septic/critically ill populations, and neither has been rehabilitated by later data. The pro-HES case above works by (accurately) distinguishing "surgical/trauma, in-label" from "septic/critically-ill" — but the claim being evaluated does not draw that line itself. A fully rigorous version of this claim would need to explicitly exclude sepsis and severe critical illness.
- Coagulation/viscoelastic studies (independent of the outcome meta-analyses above) do show HES weakens clot formation biophysically, even where the large meta-analyses find the downstream transfusion-volume effect to be small.
- The Joachim Boldt fraud scandal (>90 retracted HES studies) is a real, serious scar on this evidence base; it doesn't invalidate PHOENICS or Sessler 2026 (both post-date and were designed independent of Boldt's retracted work), but it is part of why regulatory trust in HES evidence remains lower than the raw numbers alone would suggest.
- The FLASH trial and some cardiac-surgery-specific meta-analyses have reported detrimental signals for HES in subsets of perioperative/ICU use, and EMA's 2022 direction of travel has been toward tightening rather than loosening restrictions on the product class overall, even as PHOENICS was reporting favorably — regulatory sentiment has not caught up to (or has not been convinced by) the newest surgical-population data.
- Older high-molecular-weight, high-substitution HES formulations (the ones responsible for the worst historical safety data) are meaningfully different from modern 130/0.4 tetrastarch; conflating them would overstate the case, and the evidence above is specific to the modern formulation.

**Bottom line:** for hydroxyethyl starch 130/0.4, used within labeled dosing limits, in surgical and trauma-hemorrhage patients without significant pre-existing renal disease, the claim of "safe and effective, comparable to or better than crystalloid" is well supported by the most recent and largest evidence available (PHOENICS 2025/26; Sessler et al. 2026; Chappell et al. 2021), reinforced by a plausible, measured physiological mechanism and a positive trauma RCT. Extended without qualification to the broader "critically ill" population named in the claim — especially sepsis — the case is materially weaker and conflicts with two major RCTs (6S, CHEST) that have not been overturned. The strongest honest version of this case is real and evidence-dense, but it is narrower than the claim as literally stated.
