# Fact-check: Vitamin K2 (menatetrenone) and fracture risk in elderly / stroke / Parkinson's patients with reduced mobility

## CLAIM
"Vitamin K2 (menatetrenone) supplementation reduces fracture risk in elderly patients and stroke/Parkinson's patients with reduced mobility."

## VERDICT: FALSE (for the neurological/immobility-specific part) / UNCERTAIN-LEANING-FALSE (for the general elderly population part)

The claim, as stated, is not supported by trustworthy current evidence. The specific evidentiary basis that would support the "stroke/Parkinson's patients with reduced mobility" portion of the claim comes almost entirely from a body of research that has been **formally retracted for data fabrication**. The broader claim about vitamin K2/menatetrenone reducing fracture risk in the general elderly population is, at best, inconsistent and geographically confined (positive signal largely limited to Japanese trials at pharmacologic doses; null results common outside Japan), and Western clinical guideline bodies explicitly do not recommend it.

## Full Reasoning

### 1. The specific "stroke/Parkinson's/reduced mobility" evidence base is fraudulent, not just weak

The claim closely mirrors a specific line of research by Japanese physician **Yoshihiro Sato** (Mitate Hospital) and collaborators (including Kei Satoh), who published a series of trials through the late 1990s–2000s claiming menatetrenone (vitamin K2, MK-4, typically 45 mg/day) dramatically reduced non-vertebral and hip fractures in immobilized/neurological populations:
- Stroke/hemiplegic patients (Sato et al., *Bone* 1998 — "Menatetrenone ameliorates osteopenia in disuse-affected limbs of vitamin D- and K-deficient stroke patients")
- Parkinson's disease patients (Sato et al., *Bone* 2002 — "Amelioration of osteoporosis by menatetrenone in elderly female Parkinson's disease patients with vitamin D deficiency")
- Alzheimer's disease patients (Sato et al., *Bone* 2005 — "Menatetrenone and vitamin D2 with calcium supplements prevent nonvertebral fracture in elderly women with Alzheimer's disease")

These three trials were pooled in a 2009 meta-analysis (Iwamoto et al., *Clinical Drug Investigation*) that reported strikingly large relative-risk reductions for non-vertebral fracture (RR 0.13) and hip fracture (RR 0.14) with menatetrenone in neurological-disease patients.

**All of this has since unraveled.** Sato became the subject of one of the largest research-integrity scandals in bone/orthopedic medicine:
- The 1998 stroke trial (*Bone* 1998;23:291–6) was **retracted in 2019** (retraction notice: *Bone* 2019;124:167).
- The 2005 Alzheimer's trial (*Bone*) was **retracted**, with Sato himself acknowledging it contained fabricated clinical-trial data and that co-authors had been listed without their consent.
- The 2002 Parkinson's disease trial (*Bone* 2002;31:114–8) is one of the two studies later confirmed as "problematic/retracted" and used to invalidate the downstream meta-analysis.
- Related Sato papers in *Stroke*, *Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation*, and *Journal of the Neurological Sciences* covering vitamin K/D status and fracture risk in stroke, Alzheimer's, and Parkinson's patients were also retracted (citing fabricated/implausible data, lack of credible IRB review, and authorship irregularities).
- As a direct consequence, the 2009 Iwamoto et al. meta-analysis that had pooled the stroke, Parkinson's, and Alzheimer's trials was itself **retracted in 2018/2019**, with the retraction notice stating plainly: "two of the three studies included in the meta-analysis... have been retracted due to fabrication of the data, which has rendered the results of this meta-analysis invalid."
- Retraction Watch documented that an independent statistical analysis cast doubt on more than 30 of Sato's trials; he had already retracted a dozen papers for reasons ranging from fabricated data to unauthorized co-authorship to self-plagiarism before the wider pattern was exposed.

In short: essentially the entire quantitative basis for "K2 reduces fractures in stroke/Parkinson's patients with reduced mobility" traces back to one discredited research group, and the key papers plus the meta-analysis synthesizing them have all been withdrawn from the literature. There is no credible replacement body of evidence establishing this specific sub-claim. I found no intact, non-retracted RCT demonstrating that MK-4/menatetrenone reduces fracture incidence specifically in stroke or Parkinson's patients with reduced mobility.

### 2. The Sato contamination also compromised general "elderly/postmenopausal" fracture meta-analyses

This is not confined to the neurological-disease literature. The influential 2006 Cockayne et al. meta-analysis in *Archives of Internal Medicine* (now *JAMA Internal Medicine*), "Vitamin K and the Prevention of Fractures: Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials," was flagged in a 2018 "Revised Meta-analysis of Vitamin K and Fractures" exchange: of the 7 trials contributing fracture data, **3 came from the same (Sato) center** and have since been found to be problematic/retracted. A correction added a sensitivity analysis excluding those trials; the correspondence (Torgerson's letter of caution and the authors' reply) acknowledges the original "large effect" (odds ratio ~0.30) was driven by data now considered unreliable. This means even the frequently-cited "vitamin K reduces fractures" headline meta-analysis result for the general/elderly population was substantially inflated by the same fabricated data.

### 3. What legitimate, non-retracted evidence in the general elderly/postmenopausal population shows

Multiple systematic reviews/meta-analyses not reliant on the Sato trials show a mixed, geographically patterned picture:
- A 2022 meta-analysis (Frontiers in Public Health, 16 RCTs, n=6,425 postmenopausal women) found **no significant overall reduction in fracture incidence** with vitamin K2 (RR 0.96, p=0.65); a significant effect only emerged after excluding one "heterogeneous" outlier study — a result sensitive to post-hoc exclusion decisions and not a robust primary finding.
- Reviews note that pharmacologic-dose MK-4 (45 mg/day) trials showing benefit are concentrated in **Japanese cohorts**, while trials conducted **outside Japan** (Western/Caucasian populations) have more consistently shown **null results** for BMD and fracture outcomes, even when biochemical markers of vitamin K status (undercarboxylated osteocalcin) improve.
- A placebo-controlled trial adding vitamin K1 or MK-4 on top of bisphosphonate therapy found **no significant difference** in BMD changes at hip, femoral neck, or spine versus placebo.
- Cochrane-indexed and other systematic reviews generally conclude the evidence is "inconclusive" or that "further studies are required," not that benefit is established.

### 4. Guideline bodies' position

The Bone Health & Osteoporosis Foundation (successor to the U.S. National Osteoporosis Foundation) states explicitly: "At this time, research does not support the practice of taking vitamin K supplements to prevent osteoporosis and broken bones," and recommends getting vitamin K from food rather than supplements pending more research. U.S., Canadian, and UK (NICE) guidelines do not recommend vitamin K1 or K2 supplementation for osteoporosis/fracture prevention. Japan is the notable exception, where menatetrenone is an approved treatment for osteoporosis — a regulatory status substantially built on the now-retracted Sato-era evidence base, which is itself a point of ongoing controversy in the literature.

### 5. Conclusion

- **Stroke/Parkinson's patients with reduced mobility:** The specific claim is **FALSE** as evidence-based — the trials that generated this claim have been retracted for data fabrication, and the meta-analysis synthesizing them was retracted as a direct consequence. No credible replacement evidence for this specific population was found.
- **General elderly population:** The claim is **UNCERTAIN, leaning FALSE/unsupported** — the most-cited positive evidence (Cockayne et al. meta-analysis) was substantially built on the same contaminated data; independent, non-Japanese trials and more recent meta-analyses show inconsistent-to-null effects on fracture incidence; major guideline bodies (US, Canada, UK) do not recommend vitamin K2 supplementation for fracture prevention.

Overall, this claim should be treated as **not supported by reliable current evidence**, and its most specific component (stroke/Parkinson's, reduced mobility) rests on fraudulent research that has been formally withdrawn from the scientific record.
