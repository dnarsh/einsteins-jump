# Fact-check: Hydroxyethyl starch (HES) for fluid resuscitation

**Claim under review:** "Hydroxyethyl starch solutions are a safe and effective choice for fluid resuscitation in critically ill and surgical patients, with outcomes comparable to or better than crystalloid fluids."

## Verdict: FALSE (for the critically ill population; the surgical-patient portion of the claim is also not supported as stated — see nuance below)

The claim is false as a general statement. Current medical evidence — multiple large randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews/meta-analyses, and regulatory actions by both the FDA and EMA — shows that HES solutions are **not** safe in critically ill patients (including sepsis/septic shock and general ICU populations). HES use in this population is associated with a statistically significant **increase in mortality**, **increased risk of acute kidney injury (AKI)**, and **increased need for renal replacement therapy (RRT)**, compared with crystalloids. This is not a matter of ongoing scientific dispute — it is settled well enough that HES carries a boxed (black box) warning against use in critically ill adults in the US, and HES products for infusion have been suspended from the EU market entirely.

The claim's inclusion of "surgical patients" is more nuanced but still does not support "safe and effective... comparable to or better than crystalloids" as a blanket statement: the FDA expanded its boxed warning to cover surgical and trauma patients in 2021, citing bleeding risk in cardiac surgery patients on cardiopulmonary bypass and renal risk more broadly, even though some restricted-duration perioperative meta-analyses have found less clear-cut harm than in the critically ill/septic subgroup.

## Reasoning and evidence

### 1. Landmark randomized controlled trials
Two pivotal, large RCTs form the backbone of current understanding:
- **6S trial (Scandinavian Starch for Severe Sepsis/Septic Shock)** and **CHEST trial (Crystalloid versus Hydroxyethyl Starch Trial)** — both found that resuscitation of critically ill/septic patients with 6% HES was associated with increased AKI and, in 6S, increased mortality and increased RRT use, compared with crystalloids (Ringer's acetate or saline). See NEJM: [Hydroxyethyl Starch or Saline for Fluid Resuscitation in Intensive Care](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1209759).
- The VISEP trial (cited in FDA's safety review) found harm with a hyperoncotic HES formulation in septic patients at high doses.

### 2. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses
- **Zarychanski et al., JAMA (2013)** — the benchmark systematic review/meta-analysis: IV HES for volume resuscitation in critically ill patients is not associated with reduced mortality, and once trials with questionable/retracted data (the Boldt trials) are excluded, HES is associated with a **significant increase in risk of death and AKI**. Referenced via: [ACP Journal Club summary](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23778928/), [Meta-analyses of HES for volume resuscitation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23736722/).
- **Johansen et al., Acta Anaesthesiologica Scandinavica (2021)** — systematic review with meta-analysis on HES in sepsis research: after more than a decade of use, HES 130/0.38–0.45 was found to carry serious adverse events (bleeding, AKI, death), leading to it being discouraged in clinical practice. [Link](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/aas.13954) / [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34309830/).
- A ScienceDirect meta-analysis specifically in sepsis patients: HES resuscitation associated with increased incidence of AKI and use of RRT — [Fluid resuscitation with hydroxyethyl starches in patients with sepsis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0883944113003870).
- **Nuance for surgical (non-critically-ill) patients:** A systematic review/meta-analysis of HES 130/0.4 for volume replacement in surgical patients found no significant difference in postoperative mortality or renal dysfunction versus other fluids ([PMC8111748](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8111748/)), and a more recent 2025/2026 meta-analysis (following up on a 2021 perioperative meta-analysis, adding 45 trials/6,895 patients) found that in cardiac surgery patients with HES use **restricted to under 24 hours**, HES 130/0.4 or 130/0.42 did not significantly increase AKI risk (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.91–1.16) ([Hydroxyethyl Starch and Perioperative Complications, PMC13460315](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13460315/)). This is the only pocket of evidence that could be read as supporting "comparable" outcomes — but it applies to a narrow, tightly restricted perioperative context, not a blanket endorsement, and it does not extend to bleeding risk (see below) or to critically ill patients.
- A separate meta-analysis on postoperative death and AKI with 6% HES found safety concerns persist even in some surgical contexts: [BJA — Incidence of postoperative death and AKI associated with IV 6% HES use](https://www.bjanaesthesia.org/article/S0007-0912(17)31966-9/fulltext).

### 3. Regulatory actions
- **FDA (2013):** Concluded HES solutions "should not be used in critically ill adult patients, including patients with sepsis and those admitted to the ICU," and mandated a **Boxed Warning** covering risk of mortality and severe renal injury requiring RRT. Source: [ASFA summary of FDA black box warning](https://www.apheresis.org/news/269049/US-Food-and-Drug-Administration-adds-black-boxed-warning-to-Hydroxyethyl-starch-HES.htm); [Medscape — FDA Warns of Increased Mortality Risk With HES Solution](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/806826); [PulmCCM — FDA warns against use of Hetastarch in ICU](https://www.pulmccm.org/p/fda-warns-against-use-of-hetastarch-in-icu).
- **FDA (2021):** Expanded the boxed warning to **surgical and trauma patients**, also citing increased bleeding risk in cardiac surgery patients on cardiopulmonary bypass. Source: [Hydroxyethyl Starch and Perioperative Complications, PMC13460315](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13460315/); [Comparison of HES regulatory summaries from FDA and EMA, PMC5359805](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5359805/).
- **EMA (2013, 2018, 2022):** Restricted HES use progressively — in 2013 added warnings/restrictions; in 2018 restricted use to accredited hospitals with trained staff; and in **February 2022, EMA's PRAC recommended full market suspension** of HES solutions for infusion across the EU, based on continued evidence of kidney injury and death in vulnerable patients and continued poor adherence to prescribing restrictions despite years of risk-minimization efforts. The European Commission confirmed this suspension on 24 May 2022. Sources: [EMA — Hydroxyethyl-starch solutions for infusion recommended for suspension from the market](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/news/hydroxyethyl-starch-solutions-infusion-recommended-suspension-market); [EMA DHPC](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/dhpc/hydroxyethyl-starch-hes-solutions-infusion-suspension-marketing-authorisations-due-continued-use); [FAMHP PRAC Feb 2022 summary](https://www.famhp.be/en/news/prac_february_2022_recommendation_of_suspending_hydroxyethyl_starch_solutions_for_infusion).

### 4. Clinical practice guidelines
- **Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) / Society of Critical Care Medicine, 2021 guidelines (unchanged in substance from 2016):** "For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we recommend **against** using starches for resuscitation" — a **strong recommendation** based on **high-quality evidence**. Crystalloids (with balanced crystalloids preferred) are recommended as first-line resuscitation fluid instead. Sources: [SCCM Surviving Sepsis Campaign Adult Guidelines](https://sccm.org/survivingsepsiscampaign/guidelines-and-resources/surviving-sepsis-campaign-adult-guidelines); [AAFP summary of 2021 update](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2022/1100/practice-guidelines-sepsis.html); [UIC Drug Information Group FAQ on 2021 pharmacotherapy updates](https://dig.pharmacy.uic.edu/faqs/2022-2/march-2022-faqs/update-what-are-the-2021-pharmacotherapy-updates-to-the-surviving-sepsis-campaign-international-guidelines-for-management-of-sepsis-and-septic-shock/).
- This "strong recommendation, high-quality evidence" designation is one of the more definitive stances in the SSC guidelines and reflects broad consensus in critical-care medicine.

## Synthesis

Putting the pieces together:
1. **Critically ill patients (ICU, sepsis, septic shock):** The claim is clearly **false**. Landmark RCTs (6S, CHEST, VISEP), a benchmark JAMA meta-analysis (Zarychanski 2013), subsequent meta-analyses (e.g., Johansen 2021), FDA's boxed warning (2013), EMA's full market suspension (2022), and the Surviving Sepsis Campaign's strong "recommend against" (2021, high-quality evidence) all converge on the same conclusion: HES increases mortality and AKI/RRT risk relative to crystalloids in this population, and crystalloids are the recommended first-line fluid.
2. **Surgical patients:** The picture is more mixed but still does not support the claim's "safe and effective... comparable to or better than crystalloids" framing as a general statement. Some perioperative meta-analyses (especially with HES use restricted to short courses, e.g., <24h in cardiac surgery) have not found a significant excess of AKI, but the FDA still expanded its boxed warning to cover surgical and trauma patients in 2021, citing both renal risk and increased bleeding risk in cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass. This is a regulatory judgment that residual harm signals (particularly bleeding) persist even where renal outcomes look more reassuring in narrowly restricted use cases. It is not accurate to characterize surgical use as unambiguously safe and comparable/superior to crystalloids.

Overall, the claim significantly misrepresents the current evidence base and current regulatory/clinical guidance. The correct, evidence-based position is essentially the opposite: **balanced crystalloids are preferred over HES for resuscitation in critically ill patients, and HES carries well-documented risks of AKI, RRT need, increased mortality (in the critically ill), and bleeding (perioperatively) that have led both the FDA and EMA to restrict or suspend its use.**
