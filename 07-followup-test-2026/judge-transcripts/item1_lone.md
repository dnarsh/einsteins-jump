# Fact-check: Vitamin K2 (menatetrenone) and fracture risk in elderly / stroke / Parkinson's patients

## CLAIM
"Vitamin K2 (menatetrenone) supplementation reduces fracture risk in elderly patients and stroke/Parkinson's patients with reduced mobility."

## VERDICT: FALSE (as commonly stated) — more precisely, UNSUPPORTED / NOT RELIABLY ESTABLISHED, and the specific evidence base cited for this claim is fraudulent

The claim, as it is usually presented in supplement marketing and some older review articles, rests almost entirely on a body of research that has been formally retracted for data fabrication. Once the fraudulent studies are removed, no reliable evidence currently supports a fracture-risk-reduction benefit of menatetrenone specifically in elderly, stroke, or Parkinson's disease populations with reduced mobility. Independent, non-retracted meta-analyses of menatetrenone in osteoporosis more broadly find it may modestly improve bone mineral density (BMD) and biochemical markers, but conclude that its effect on actual fracture incidence is "uncertain."

## REASONING

### 1. The specific claim traces back almost entirely to Yoshihiro Sato's research group — now known to be fraudulent

The claim about menatetrenone reducing fractures specifically in stroke and Parkinson's disease patients with reduced mobility comes from a series of trials conducted by Yoshihiro Sato and colleagues in Japan in the late 1990s and 2000s:

- **Stroke patients (Sato et al., Bone, 1998):** "Menatetrenone ameliorates osteopenia in disuse-affected limbs of vitamin D- and K-deficient stroke patients." Reported that in 108 hemiplegic stroke patients, BMD on the hemiplegic side increased 4.3% in the MK-4 (45 mg/day) group vs. a 4.7% decrease in untreated controls over 12 months.
  - **RETRACTED.** Retraction notice: Bone 2019;124:167, for data fabrication.
- **Parkinson's disease patients (Sato et al., Bone, 2002):** "Amelioration of osteoporosis by menatetrenone in elderly female Parkinson's disease patients with vitamin D deficiency." Reported BMD improvements and reduced nonvertebral fracture incidence with 45 mg/day MK-4 over 12 months in 120 patients.
  - **RETRACTED.** Retraction notice: Bone 2018;106:212. Sato himself informed the journal the paper contained fabricated clinical trial data; he also stated he had listed co-authors without their consent.
- **Alzheimer's disease patients (Sato et al.):** A related trial combining menatetrenone/risedronate/calcium in Alzheimer's patients (Kurume Med J. 2011) was also **retracted** for scientific misconduct.
- **Meta-analysis synthesizing all three (Iwamoto & Sato, Clinical Drug Investigation, 2009):** "Efficacy of Menatetrenone (Vitamin K2) against Non-Vertebral and Hip Fractures in Patients with Neurological Diseases: meta-analysis of three randomized, controlled trials." This pooled the stroke, Parkinson's, and Alzheimer's trials above and reported dramatic risk reductions (relative risks of 0.13–0.14 for non-vertebral/hip fractures).
  - **RETRACTED** (Clinical Drug Investigation, retraction note 2018) because two of the three included trials were themselves retracted for data fabrication, invalidating the pooled result.

### 2. Scale of the fraud

Yoshihiro Sato (died 2017) is one of the most prolific cases of documented research fraud in medicine. Investigators from the University of Auckland and University of Aberdeen reviewed ~292 of his papers; the resulting fallout produced roughly 121 retractions across Sato and his frequent co-author Jun Iwamoto (43 attributed to Sato alone, placing him high on Retraction Watch's all-time leaderboard). Sato admitted before his death that he had falsified data. Investigators found implausible/impossible patient recruitment timelines (e.g., enlisting 500+ patients from a single village in two months) and fabricated outcomes across trials spanning vitamin D, vitamin K, and osteoporosis drugs in stroke, Parkinson's, and Alzheimer's populations — i.e., exactly the "elderly with reduced mobility" population the claim targets.

Critically, follow-up analysis found that Sato's fabricated vitamin K studies had real-world downstream effects: a review built on his work was the basis for 2011 and 2015 Japanese clinical guidelines recommending vitamin K2 for fracture-risk patients, and once his studies were excluded, the reported benefit became statistically non-significant. Even years after retraction, his papers continued to be cited uncritically in 88 subsequent systematic reviews/guidelines through 2020, because many retractions were not flagged clearly by citing authors.

### 3. What remains once the fraudulent data is excluded

Independent, non-Sato meta-analyses of menatetrenone in osteoporosis (a broader population, not specifically stroke/Parkinson's) give a much more modest and uncertain picture:

- **Su et al., Osteoporosis International (2019, corrected 2021), "The efficacy and safety of menatetrenone in the management of osteoporosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs."** Found menatetrenone decreased undercarboxylated osteocalcin and modestly improved lumbar spine BMD versus placebo, but explicitly concluded: **"its benefit in fracture risk control was uncertain."**
- **Huang et al., Osteoporosis International (2015)** and a 2022 Frontiers in Public Health meta-analysis of vitamin K2 in postmenopausal osteoporosis found mixed results on fracture incidence — one analysis found no significant fracture reduction until a "heterogeneous" outlier study was excluded post hoc, after which a reduction became significant (RR 0.43) — a result that should be treated cautiously given it required excluding a study to reach significance, and such analyses still risk carrying contaminated Sato-derived data forward through citation chains.
- No independent, non-retracted RCT specifically re-establishes a fracture-risk benefit of menatetrenone in stroke or Parkinson's disease patients with reduced mobility. The entire evidence base for that specific sub-claim is the retracted Sato trials.

### 4. Biological plausibility vs. clinical proof

There is a plausible mechanistic rationale: immobilized/disabled elderly patients (post-stroke, Parkinson's) often have poor nutritional status, vitamin D and K deficiency, and accelerated disuse-related bone loss, which independently raises fracture risk. Vitamin K's role in gamma-carboxylation of osteocalcin gives a biologically coherent story for why K2 could help bone metabolism. However, biological plausibility is not clinical proof, and the specific clinical trials that were supposed to supply that proof for this population were fabricated.

## BOTTOM LINE

- The claim as commonly circulated is **not supported by valid evidence** — its key supporting trials (Sato et al. 1998 stroke study; Sato et al. 2002 Parkinson's study; the 2009 pooled meta-analysis) have all been **formally retracted for fabricated data**.
- More broadly (outside the stroke/Parkinson's-specific claim), independent, non-fraudulent meta-analyses of menatetrenone in osteoporosis find modest BMD/biomarker benefits but describe the effect on actual fracture incidence as **"uncertain."**
- Verdict: **FALSE / evidence-fabricated** for the specific stroke/Parkinson's fracture-reduction claim; **UNCERTAIN at best** for general elderly fracture-risk reduction based on the remaining legitimate literature.
