# Fact-check: Hydroxyethyl starch (HES) for fluid resuscitation

**Claim under review:** "Hydroxyethyl starch solutions are a safe and effective choice for fluid resuscitation in critically ill and surgical patients, with outcomes comparable to or better than crystalloid fluids."

## Verdict: FALSE (as a general claim spanning both populations)

The claim is **false for critically ill patients** (strong, high-certainty evidence of harm) and **not supported / overstated for surgical patients** (best available evidence shows non-inferiority on a narrow renal-injury endpoint under restricted short-duration use, not superiority or general "comparable-or-better" outcomes; regulators still treat HES as higher-risk than crystalloids in this group too). No high-quality trial or meta-analysis demonstrates HES is *better* than crystalloids on any hard clinical outcome (mortality, renal replacement therapy, bleeding), and the critical-care literature shows it is often worse.

---

## 1. Critically ill / sepsis patients — evidence of harm is strong and consistent

- **6S trial (Scandinavian Starch for Severe Sepsis/Septic Shock), NEJM 2012** — RCT of 798 ICU patients with severe sepsis randomized to 6% HES 130/0.42 vs. Ringer's acetate. HES significantly *increased* 90-day mortality and risk of renal replacement therapy (RRT) compared to a balanced crystalloid. (Perner et al., NEJM 2012; https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1204242)
- **CHEST trial, NEJM 2012** — 7,000 general ICU patients randomized to 6% HES 130/0.4 vs. saline. No significant 90-day mortality difference (18.0% vs 17.0%), but HES significantly increased use of renal replacement therapy (RR 1.21, 95% CI 1.00–1.45, p=0.04). (Myburgh et al., NEJM 2012; https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1209759)
- **Zarychanski et al., JAMA 2013** — systematic review/meta-analysis of RCTs in critically ill patients: HES associated with increased mortality and acute kidney injury/need for RRT versus other resuscitation fluids.
- **2025 network meta-analysis of sepsis fluid resuscitation** (PubMed, EMBASE, WOS through Sept 2024; 32 RCTs) found balanced crystalloids had the lowest all-cause mortality and highest SUCRA ranking (83.1%) among all fluid options compared, including HES — i.e., HES did not outperform crystalloids on survival. (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12209188/)
- A high-quality systematic review cited by the Scandinavian Society of Anaesthesiology (42 RCTs, 11,399 patients) found HES products associated with a 59% increased risk of acute kidney injury and 32% increased risk of renal replacement therapy compared with other IV fluids.

## 2. Regulatory actions — consistent restriction, not endorsement

- **FDA, June 2013:** Issued a **Boxed (black box) Warning** on HES products (Hespan, Hextend, Voluven, generics) after review of RCTs, meta-analyses, and observational data: "Do not use HES products… in critically ill adult patients, including patients with sepsis." Warned of increased mortality, renal injury (requiring monitoring of renal function for 90 days), and increased bleeding risk in cardiac surgery/CPB patients. (https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/safety-availability-biologics/labeling-changes-mortality-kidney-injury-and-excess-bleeding-hydroxyethyl-starch-products; https://www.apheresis.org/news/269049/US-Food-and-Drug-Administration-adds-black-boxed-warning-to-Hydroxyethyl-starch-HES.htm)
- **FDA, 2021:** Expanded the boxed warning to cover **surgical and trauma patients**, not just ICU/sepsis patients (per the 2026 Sessler et al. meta-analysis background section, PMC13460315).
- **EMA, 2013:** Restricted HES use in critically ill patients, those with sepsis, burns, or renal impairment; retained only a narrow indication for hypovolemia from acute blood loss when crystalloids alone are insufficient.
- **EMA PRAC/CMDh, January 2018:** Recommended full **suspension** of HES marketing authorization across the EU, because the 2013 restrictions were not being followed in practice (drug utilization studies showed continued use in contraindicated sepsis/critically-ill populations). This was contested by some national anesthesia societies (e.g., DGAI) but fully supported by others (e.g., SSAI, citing the AKI/RRT risk data above). (https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/news/hydroxyethyl-starch-solutions-infusion-be-suspended-cmdh-endorses-prac-recommendation; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/aas.13120)
- Interim EU measures (2018) imposed a controlled-access program (mandatory prescriber training, supply restricted to approved centers) rather than full withdrawal; per the 2026 Sessler meta-analysis, the **EMA initiated full withdrawal of HES in 2022**.
- **Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) guidelines** — since 2016, maintained through the 2021 update and the forthcoming 2026 update: **"For adults with sepsis or septic shock, we recommend against using starches for resuscitation." Certainty of evidence: High.** This is one of the strongest, most consistent recommendations in the guideline document. (https://sccm.org/survivingsepsiscampaign; 2021 update: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00134-021-06506-y)

## 3. The Boldt research-fraud scandal — a major confound in the "pro-HES" literature

Joachim Boldt, a prominent German anesthesiologist and HES advocate, was found to have fabricated data across ~90 of the studies reviewed (out of >100 examined), many specifically supporting HES safety/efficacy in surgical fluid management. This was described as one of the largest research-fraud scandals in medical history. Critically, meta-analyses that exclude Boldt's fabricated trials show HES associated with significantly increased risk of death and acute kidney injury compared with other resuscitation fluids — i.e., **Boldt's studies were essentially the only ones showing benefit, and they were fake.** His narrative reviews (built on his own fabricated primary data) continue to be cited today. (Wikipedia "Joachim Boldt"; PMC3327055 "Fraud in anaesthetic research and publication"; retractionwatch.com coverage)

This matters directly for the claim: much of the historical evidence base that once made HES look "safe and effective" relative to crystalloids has since been shown to rest partly on fraudulent data.

## 4. Surgical/perioperative patients — more contested, recent partial reassurance, but not "better than crystalloids"

This is the one area where the evidence is genuinely more nuanced and where the claim has *some* current support, though still short of what's asserted:

- **Sessler et al., Anesthesiology 2026;145(3):591–603** ("Hydroxyethyl Starch and Perioperative Complications: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis") — 114 trials, 13,951 surgical patients (cardiac 30%, major abdominal 46%, other 24%), almost all with HES exposure restricted to <24 hours. Found HES 130/0.4/0.42 did **not** significantly increase acute kidney injury (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.91–1.16) or worsen creatinine change, and did not meaningfully worsen other adverse events or mortality. Conclusion: "Current hydroxyethyl starch solutions do not provoke renal injury in surgical patients." (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13460315/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42160629/)
- An accompanying editorial, **London, Myles & Skubas, Anesthesiology 2026;145(3):543–547**, "Reconsidering Hydroxyethyl Starch in Contemporary Perioperative Care," discusses these findings as grounds to revisit blanket restrictions in short-duration elective surgical use — but this is a reconsideration/discussion piece, not a new guideline change, and it is explicitly limited to short-duration (<24h), modern tetrastarch (130/0.4) use in surgery, not general ICU/critical-care or sepsis use. (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42579599/)
- Even in this more favorable surgical dataset, the finding is **non-inferiority on renal injury**, not superiority over crystalloids, and mortality/other hard outcomes were "not meaningfully worsened" (again, not shown to be improved).
- Coagulopathy/bleeding: mixed by HES generation. Older, higher-molecular-weight starches (>200kDa) show fairly consistent signals for increased blood loss/transfusion vs. crystalloids/other colloids. Newer tetrastarches (130/0.4) show more comparable safety in some RCTs (e.g., a cardiac-surgery meta-analysis found no clear safety issue with tetrastarch vs. other fluids on blood loss/transfusion/LOS), but retrospective/institutional data and FDA safety communications continue to flag coagulopathy signals with modern HES in cardiac surgery, and the FDA boxed warning specifically warns against HES use in cardiopulmonary bypass surgery due to excess bleeding risk. (PMC4301454; FDA labeling communication)
- The FDA's 2021 expansion of the boxed warning to surgical and trauma patients, and the EMA's 2022 move to fully withdraw HES from the market EU-wide, indicate that regulators — reviewing the same totality of evidence up through 2021-2022 — did **not** conclude HES was safe and effective with outcomes comparable-or-better than crystalloids in surgical patients either. The 2026 Sessler meta-analysis is recent enough that it has not (yet) reversed these regulatory positions; it's presented as prompting "reconsideration," not as settled new consensus.

## Bottom line

1. **Critically ill / septic patients:** The claim is directly contradicted by multiple large RCTs (6S, CHEST), meta-analyses (Zarychanski 2013, 2025 network meta-analysis), and the highest-certainty (SSC "High" certainty) guideline recommendation *against* HES use, plus FDA boxed-warning contraindication and EMA restriction/withdrawal. HES increases renal replacement therapy need and, in sepsis specifically, increases mortality — the opposite of "safe and effective... comparable to or better than crystalloids."
2. **Surgical patients:** More contested. A 2026 meta-analysis offers reassurance specifically on renal injury for short-duration (<24h), modern tetrastarch use, prompting active reconsideration in the field — but (a) this doesn't establish HES is *better* than crystalloids on any outcome, only non-inferior on one endpoint; (b) coagulopathy/bleeding concerns persist, especially in cardiac surgery; (c) regulators (FDA 2021, EMA 2022) still treat HES as higher-risk than crystalloids in surgical/trauma populations as of their most recent formal actions.
3. **Historical confound:** A substantial part of the original "pro-HES" evidence base was fabricated by Joachim Boldt; once fraudulent trials are excluded, the literature skews toward harm or, at best, no benefit over crystalloids.

Given the claim asserts blanket safety/efficacy *and* superiority-or-equivalence to crystalloids across **both** critically ill and surgical patients, and the strongest, most consistent, least-contested evidence (in the critically-ill/septic population) shows clear harm, the claim as stated should be rated **FALSE**. At most, a narrow, heavily-caveated version of the claim ("modern HES 130/0.4 used briefly and only in elective surgical patients does not appear to cause more renal injury than crystalloids") has emerging, contested support — but that is a much narrower claim than the one being fact-checked.
